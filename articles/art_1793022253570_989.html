Andy Burnham Refuses to Rule Out Referendum on EU Freedom of Movement

The Prime Minister has left open the possibility of a national referendum on reinstating freedom of movement with the European Union, insisting that his administration will "never move ahead of the British public when it comes to Brexit."

When asked whether free movement could be reintroduced without a direct public mandate, Andy Burnham answered in the negative. While government insiders caution that formal policy mechanisms remain under deliberation, the remarks signal a calculated willingness to reconsider the parameters of Britain’s post-Brexit settlement over the long term.

Burnham has firmly ruled out structural shifts to the United Kingdom’s relationship with Brussels prior to the next general election, due by 2029. Nevertheless, Downing Street plans to initiate a broader public dialogue regarding future options ahead of an upcoming European summit next month. Potential pathways under consideration reportedly range from retaining current trade arrangements to re-entering the customs union, the single market, or eventually holding a broader plebiscite on full membership.

In remarks setting the stage for the debate, the Prime Minister advocated for an "honest discussion" regarding border policy. He contended that the nature of immigration had shifted detrimentally since the UK’s departure from the bloc—moving from temporary, younger European workers who frequently returned home to higher levels of permanent migration from further afield. Burnham also noted that leaving the EU stripped Britain of vital returns agreements, which he argued directly contributed to the ongoing small boats crisis in the English Channel.

Crucially, Burnham established clear boundaries around future integration, confirming he would never advocate for joining the Schengen passport-free area or abandoning the pound for the euro.

The Prime Minister’s comments provoked an immediate backlash from political rivals. Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch warned that voters backed Brexit precisely to end uncontrolled immigration, while Reform UK leader Nigel Farage denounced any move towards free movement as a "total betrayal." Conversely, the Liberal Democrats argued that dismantling barriers to mobility remains essential for reviving British economic productivity.
